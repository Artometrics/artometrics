<div id="quarto-content">
<main class="art-article-main">
<p class="art-p">Texas holds 10,741 of the 42,724 historical markers in a TidyTuesday extract of the Historical Marker Database, a share of 25.1%. Georgia, in second place, has 3,294. The country's roadside history is not spread evenly, and the dates on the markers show why: it was built in bursts.</p>
<p class="art-p">The extract covers 50 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico. The typical state has about 230 markers; Texas has more than 46 times that. This report asks where the markers are, when they went up, what they say, and how many are already gone.</p>
<h2 id="one-state-a-quarter-of-the-map" class="anchored">One state, a quarter of the map</h2>
<h3 id="one-state-a-quarter-of-the-map-look" class="anchored">One state holds a quarter of the markers</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/markers/charts/chart1_states.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Historical Marker Database via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/markers/charts/chart1_states.png" role="img" aria-label="One state holds a quarter of the markers"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">The five biggest states, Texas, Georgia, Virginia, Tennessee and California, hold <strong>52.7%</strong> of all markers. Texas alone has <strong>10,741</strong>, more than three times Georgia's <strong>3,294</strong>. </p>
<p class="art-p">The erector field points to state programs. The Texas Historical Commission is named on <strong>7,084</strong> entries, Virginia's Department of Historic Resources on <strong>1,554</strong>, the Tennessee Historical Commission on <strong>1,432</strong> and the Georgia Historical Commission on <strong>1,410</strong>. A state that funds markers for decades builds a dense record.</p>
<h2 id="two-years-that-built-the-record" class="anchored">Two years that built the record</h2>
<h3 id="two-years-that-built-the-record-look" class="anchored">Marker-building spikes in 1890 and 1936</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/markers/charts/chart2_years.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Historical Marker Database via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/markers/charts/chart2_years.png" role="img" aria-label="Marker-building spikes in 1890 and 1936"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">The year erected is known for <strong>34,686</strong> of the 42,724 markers. Two years tower over their neighbors. <strong>1936</strong> has <strong>887</strong> markers, and <strong>774</strong> of them are in Texas. That was the Texas Centennial: the state marked 100 years of independence with monuments, buildings and, according to the Texas State Historical Association, a marker in every county.</p>
<p class="art-p">The other spike is <strong>1890</strong>, with <strong>686</strong> markers. In the extract, <strong>625</strong> of them name the Chickamauga and Chattanooga National Military Park Commission or the later battlefield commission as the erector, and <strong>503</strong> are in Georgia and <strong>177</strong> in Tennessee. A third peak, <strong>785</strong> markers in 2003, is more spread out: Ohio accounts for 334 of them.</p>
<h2 id="the-2000s-were-the-busiest-decade" class="anchored">The 2000s were the busiest decade</h2>
<h3 id="the-2000s-were-the-busiest-decade-look" class="anchored">The 2000s built the most markers</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/markers/charts/chart3_decades.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Historical Marker Database via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/markers/charts/chart3_decades.png" role="img" aria-label="The 2000s built the most markers"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">Outside the spikes, marker-building has trended up since the 1950s. The 1950s produced <strong>2,228</strong> markers, the 1960s <strong>3,936</strong>, the 1990s <strong>4,269</strong> and the 2000s <strong>6,163</strong>, the most of any decade. The 2010s followed with <strong>5,691</strong>.</p>
<p class="art-p">The 2020s show <strong>891</strong>, but that decade runs only through 2023 in this extract. Yearly counts drop from 591 in 2019 to <strong>321</strong> in 2020, <strong>230</strong> in 2022 and just <strong>23</strong> in 2023. Recent markers are probably the least complete part of the record, so the drop should not be read as a slowdown.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-titles-say" class="anchored">What the titles say</h2>
<h3 id="what-the-titles-say-look" class="anchored">Church is the most common word on a marker</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/markers/charts/chart4_words.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Historical Marker Database via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/markers/charts/chart4_words.png" role="img" aria-label="Church is the most common word on a marker"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">Count the words in marker titles, drop filler such as <em>the</em>, <em>of</em> and <em>county</em>, and one word leads: <strong>church</strong>, in <strong>3,519</strong> titles, or 8.2% of the extract. <strong>House</strong> follows with <strong>2,437</strong>, then <strong>cemetery</strong> with <strong>1,655</strong> and <strong>school</strong> with <strong>1,236</strong>.</p>
<p class="art-p">Denominations show up as words in their own right: <strong>Baptist</strong> in 853 titles and <strong>Methodist</strong> in 789. <strong>Infantry</strong> appears in 755. The list is a quick index of what communities chose to mark: places of worship, homes, burial grounds and schools.</p>
<h2 id="markers-that-are-no-longer-there" class="anchored">Markers that are no longer there</h2>
<h3 id="markers-that-are-no-longer-there-look" class="anchored">In Washington, D.C., about one marker in seven is missing</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/markers/charts/chart5_missing.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Historical Marker Database via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/markers/charts/chart5_missing.png" role="img" aria-label="In Washington, D.C., about one marker in seven is missing"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">The database lets volunteers flag a marker as reported missing or confirmed missing. In the extract, <strong>1,181</strong> markers carry a flag: <strong>648</strong> confirmed and <strong>533</strong> reported, together <strong>2.8%</strong> of the total.</p>
<p class="art-p">The rate varies. In the District of Columbia, <strong>65</strong> of <strong>440</strong> markers are flagged, <strong>14.8%</strong>. Indiana is next among places with at least 300 markers at <strong>5.8%</strong>, then Virginia at <strong>5.7%</strong>. Virginia has the most flagged markers in absolute terms, <strong>186</strong>.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-file-cannot-tell-you" class="anchored">What this file cannot tell you</h2>
<p class="art-p">The extract describes what volunteers have catalogued, not every marker that stands. A state with an active group of contributors looks richer than one with the same number of markers and fewer volunteers. The year erected is missing for 8,038 markers. Titles are matched as whole words, so spelling variants are counted separately. The companion file of counties with no entry lists 103 places, 50 of them in Puerto Rico, 14 in Alaska and 13 in North Dakota; it marks gaps in the database, not proof that nothing is there.</p>
<p class="art-p">The reasons given for the 1890 and 1936 spikes come from the erector names in the data and from the Texas State Historical Association. The dataset itself does not record why a marker was put up.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away" class="anchored">What to take away</h2>
<p class="art-p">Roadside history in this extract is concentrated and bursty. One state holds 25.1% of the markers, one year, 1936, accounts for 887 of them, and the busiest decade is the 2000s. Church is the commonest word in a title, and about one marker in 36 is flagged missing.</p>
<p class="art-p">The pattern follows programs more than places. Where a state, a commission or an anniversary put money behind markers, the record is dense. Where nobody did, the map is thin.</p>

<h2 id="data-methods-and-sources" class="anchored">Data, methods &amp; sources</h2>
<h3 id="data-and-method" class="anchored art-back-matter__subhead">Data and method</h3>
<p class="art-p"><a href="https://github.com/rfordatascience/tidytuesday/tree/main/data/2023/2023-07-04">TidyTuesday, 2023-07-04: Historical Markers. The R for Data Science community's release of the Historical Marker Database USA index, 42,724 markers, plus a companion file of 103 counties with no entry.</a></p>
<p class="art-p">The working file is the TidyTuesday release of 2023-07-04: 42,724 rows, one per marker, with state, year erected, erector, coordinates, title and a missing flag. Counts are exact counts of rows. Years use only rows with a year erected. Title words are matched case-insensitively on whole words and counted once per marker.</p>
<p class="art-p">Charts are drawn in R with the Artometrics editorial theme from <code>scripts/r/artometrics_editorial.R</code>. The analysis script is <code>articles/markers/analysis.R</code>, and the tables behind every chart are in the downloads. The cover art plots every marker at its coordinates.</p>
