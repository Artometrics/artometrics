<div id="quarto-content">
<main class="art-article-main">
<p class="art-p">In the 23 states with polling-place data for every general election from 2012 to 2020, the number of distinct polling-place addresses fell from <strong>45,512</strong> to <strong>38,850</strong>, a loss of <strong>6,662</strong>, or <strong>14.6%</strong>. Four states account for 69% of it.</p>
<p class="art-p">The data comes from the Center for Public Integrity's collection of state and local polling-place lists, released through TidyTuesday. It has 461,445 rows across seven election dates and 39 states. Coverage changes from year to year, so this report compares only states that appear in all five general elections: 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2020.</p>
<h2 id="four-states-did-most-of-it" class="anchored">Four states did most of it</h2>
<h3 id="four-states-did-most-of-it-look" class="anchored">Four states account for 69% of the net loss</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/polling/charts/chart1_net_change.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Center for Public Integrity via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/polling/charts/chart1_net_change.png" role="img" aria-label="Four states account for 69% of the net loss"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">Ohio lost <strong>1,264</strong> addresses between 2012 and 2020 and Maryland <strong>1,250</strong>. Indiana lost <strong>1,037</strong> and Illinois <strong>1,033</strong>. Those four add up to <strong>4,584</strong> of the net <strong>6,662</strong>, or <strong>69%</strong>. Pennsylvania, the largest state in the set with 7,454 addresses in 2012, lost <strong>695</strong>.</p>
<p class="art-p">Without Maryland, the other 22 states lost <strong>12.3%</strong> instead of 14.6%. The decline is broad, but a handful of states set the total.</p>
<h2 id="a-slow-slide-then-a-cliff" class="anchored">A slow slide, then a cliff</h2>
<h3 id="a-slow-slide-then-a-cliff-look" class="anchored">A slow slide, then a 9% drop in 2020</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/polling/charts/chart2_index.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Center for Public Integrity via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/polling/charts/chart2_index.png" role="img" aria-label="A slow slide, then a 9% drop in 2020"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">Indexed to 2012, the total reached <strong>96.7</strong> in 2014, <strong>95.9</strong> in 2016 and <strong>93.8</strong> in 2018. It then fell to <strong>85.4</strong> in 2020. The 2018 to 2020 step, from 42,695 to 38,850 addresses, is a <strong>9.0%</strong> drop in one cycle, and it is larger than the whole 2012 to 2018 decline of 6.2%.</p>
<p class="art-p">Two things overlap in 2020: long-running consolidation and the pandemic. The data records where polling places were, not why they closed.</p>
<h2 id="the-deepest-cuts-in-percent" class="anchored">The deepest cuts in percent</h2>
<h3 id="the-deepest-cuts-in-percent-look" class="anchored">Maryland and North Dakota cut deepest</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/polling/charts/chart3_pct_change.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Center for Public Integrity via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/polling/charts/chart3_pct_change.png" role="img" aria-label="Maryland and North Dakota cut deepest"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">By percentage, <strong>Maryland</strong> fell <strong>79.6%</strong> and <strong>North Dakota</strong> <strong>57.4%</strong>, from 263 addresses to 112. Indiana fell <strong>35.6%</strong> and Ohio <strong>26.7%</strong>. Indiana's drop came early: it listed 2,912 addresses in 2012 and 1,979 in 2014.</p>
<p class="art-p">Only <strong>3</strong> of the 23 states added polling places. Vermont grew from 139 to 261 (+122), Alaska from 390 to 416 (+26) and Virginia from 2,383 to 2,408 (+25). Connecticut was nearly flat, at 643 and 638.</p>
<h2 id="maryland-from-1566-to-320" class="anchored">Maryland: from 1,566 to 320</h2>
<h3 id="maryland-from-1566-to-320-look" class="anchored">Maryland went from 1,566 places to 320 in one cycle</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/polling/charts/chart4_maryland.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Center for Public Integrity via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/polling/charts/chart4_maryland.png" role="img" aria-label="Maryland went from 1,566 places to 320 in one cycle"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">Maryland's count sat between <strong>1,566</strong> and <strong>1,581</strong> addresses for four straight general elections, then fell to <strong>320</strong> in 2020. The reason is documented. The Maryland State Board of Elections reports that voters on Election Day 2020 used <strong>321</strong> vote centers instead of neighborhood polling places, the first time the state used vote centers on Election Day. The board gave two reasons: election judges were resigning during the public-health emergency, and many neighborhood sites could not meet social-distancing guidelines.</p>
<p class="art-p">The dataset's 320 distinct addresses is within one of the board's figure of 321. Maryland is the clearest case in the data of a state that did not close polling places one by one but replaced a system.</p>
<h2 id="the-map-keeps-changing" class="anchored">The map keeps changing</h2>
<h3 id="the-map-keeps-changing-look" class="anchored">The map keeps changing: 30 states in 2012, 35 in 2020</h3>
<figure class="art-chart">
  <div class="art-chart-live" data-chart="/data/articles/polling/charts/chart5_coverage.plotly.json" data-source="Data: Center for Public Integrity via TidyTuesday - ARTOMETRICS" data-fallback="/images/content/articles/polling/charts/chart5_coverage.png" role="img" aria-label="The map keeps changing: 30 states in 2012, 35 in 2020"></div>
</figure>
<p class="art-p">The raw row count rises from <strong>87,176</strong> in 2012 to <strong>103,290</strong> in 2020. That rise tracks coverage as much as anything: the number of states with data went from <strong>30</strong> to <strong>35</strong> over the same period. Georgia, for example, has no data before 2016 and Alabama, California, Missouri, Nevada, Texas and Utah appear only in 2020. A national total taken from this file would mix real change with changes in which states are listed.</p>
<p class="art-p">That is why the comparison in this report uses only the <strong>23</strong> states present in all five general elections. The source notes also say that Colorado, Hawaii, Oregon, Washington and Utah vote mostly by mail and have little or no data.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-file-cannot-tell-you" class="anchored">What this file cannot tell you</h2>
<p class="art-p">A polling place here is a distinct address, after trimming whitespace and ignoring case. Two lists that format the same address differently count as two places, and two precincts sharing one building count as one. Counts describe what the source lists contained; they are not official tallies. Early-voting sites and Election Day sites are mixed in some states and separate in others, and the file does not carry a consistent flag for every row.</p>
<p class="art-p">The data cannot say whether a closure made voting harder. Distance to a polling place, wait times and turnout are not in the file. A drop in addresses can reflect consolidation into larger vote centers, as in Maryland, rather than less capacity.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-take-away" class="anchored">What to take away</h2>
<p class="art-p">Across 23 states with a complete record, polling-place addresses fell 14.6% between 2012 and 2020, and 69% of that came from Ohio, Maryland, Indiana and Illinois. The step between 2018 and 2020 was the largest, and Maryland's move to vote centers is the biggest single piece.</p>
<p class="art-p">Any claim about polling places nationwide needs the same discipline as this one: pick the states that are present in every year, and compare them to themselves.</p>

<h2 id="data-methods-and-sources" class="anchored">Data, methods &amp; sources</h2>
<h3 id="data-and-method" class="anchored art-back-matter__subhead">Data and method</h3>
<p class="art-p"><a href="https://github.com/rfordatascience/tidytuesday/tree/main/data/2024/2024-01-16">TidyTuesday, 2024-01-16: US Polling Places 2012-2020. The R for Data Science community's release of the polling_places table, 461,445 rows.</a></p>
<p class="art-p">The working file is the TidyTuesday release of 2024-01-16, which has one row per polling place per election. The analysis keeps the five general elections (2012-11-06, 2014-11-04, 2016-11-08, 2018-11-06, 2020-11-03), normalizes each address to upper case without extra whitespace, and counts distinct addresses per state and election. Rows for the other election dates are left out of the comparison.</p>
<p class="art-p">Charts are drawn in R with the Artometrics editorial theme from <code>scripts/r/artometrics_editorial.R</code>. The analysis script is <code>articles/polling/analysis.R</code>, and the tables behind every chart are in the downloads. The cover art shows one dot per 100 addresses in 2012, with the dots that were gone by 2020 in pink, placed at random.</p>
