ANIME: Source Sets the Audience Floor

In a century of MyAnimeList data, light-novel TV adaptations draw 32 times the median audience of original series. The data also shows where fan ratings and commercial value part ways.

Why does one anime reach more than a million fans while another, often just as well made, reaches a few thousand? A MyAnimeList archive of 13,631 titles, from 1917 theatrical shorts to an early-2019 snapshot, points first to a decision made before any animator is hired: where the story comes from.

On television, light-novel adaptations draw a median of 153,184 platform members. Manga adaptations draw 37,500 and original series draw 4,738, which puts light novels about 32 times ahead of originals. Only 4.6 percent of originals and 0.5 percent of game adaptations score 8.0 or higher, compared with 17.0 percent of manga adaptations, 210 of 1,232. Across all scored titles, score and audience move together. The rank correlation is 0.74, and the correlation of score with the log of member count is 0.72. Every comparison is measured against the catalog median of 6.38.

This is the record of a production system. A production committee pools the budget and splits copyright. The animation studio is typically a contracted manufacturer that does not own the IP. The figures are fan behavior on one platform. They show which projects found audiences, not which ones made money.

Each format wave lines up with a new distribution channel. Original video animation ran at roughly 100 titles a year from 1990 to 1993 and peaked at 115 in 1993. The archive holds 4,260 TV titles. Web-first releases grew from 40 in 2009 to 216 in 2018. Reading those waves as responses to new channels is an editorial frame. The file cannot show who paid. The literature documents one wave directly: starting with Tetsuwan Atom in 1963, TV anime was tied to sponsor merchandising.

The source of a story is the biggest dividing line in the TV catalog. Light-novel adaptations have a median score of 7.35. Manga is close, at 7.29, and produces the highest share of 8.0s, 17.0 percent. This is selection, not proof that adapting a book lowers risk. Originals sit at 6.63, and only 4.6 percent reach 8.0. Game adaptations are weaker still: 0.5 percent, one title in 210, IDOLiSH7 in 2018. These source figures use scored television, 4,240 titles out of 4,260.

Across the 20 largest studios, medians run from 7.46 to 5.40. Bones has 115 titles, a median of 7.46, and an interquartile range of 0.95. Bones has the tightest spread among the top scorers. That 0.95 is only the 7th-tightest of all 20 studios with at least 100 titles. Kyoto Animation's 110 titles have a median of 7.43 and an IQR of 1.22. It employs mostly salaried in-house staff. OLM's 209 titles have a median of 6.64 and the tightest spread overall, an IQR of 0.70, at a lower level. DLE, with 153 titles, mostly makes short-form titles, with a median episode of 3 minutes, so its median score isn't directly comparable to studios making 24-minute episodes. The largest studio, Toei, with 737 titles, sits near the bottom of the table at 6.68. Of those titles, 2.8 percent score 8.0 or higher, 21 of 737. Studios differ clearly in their typical output, which is the better guide to a commission.

TV sequels score higher, consistent with only well-received shows getting one. The 1,084 TV sequels have a median score of 7.02, and 14.7 percent score 8.0 or higher. The 3,156 TV titles that are not sequels have a median of 6.73 and a rate of 5.9 percent. Sequels also draw more members at the median, 11,430 against 8,013. The pattern is consistent with selection, not an effect of being a sequel. It also fits the shortlist count: 30 of the 43 high scorers with at least 1,000 ratings are sequels.

Of 603 titles scoring 8.0 or higher, 78 sit beyond popularity rank 3,000. Requiring at least 1,000 ratings cuts that to 43. Of those 43, 30 are sequels; only 10 TV series or films that aren't sequels remain. That list of 10 is an editorial shortlist, not a revenue ranking.

The snapshot is early 2019. MyAnimeList is an English-language enthusiast site. Thriller titles have a median of 7.50. The file has no revenue. Source, studio, and sequel are all chosen with demand in mind. None of the gaps should be read as the effect of that choice alone.
