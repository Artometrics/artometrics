# READMITTED — 3-minute explainer (~3:00)

Medicare does not pay hospitals for keeping patients healthy after discharge. It pays for admissions — then docks up to three percent of every Medicare dollar if too many patients boomerang within thirty days. That is the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, live since October twenty-twelve. Fourteen years in, the latest CMS extract still shows forty-eight point two percent of hospital–condition pairs above the one-point-zero excess readmission ratio. The penalty did not erase readmissions. It made them measurable.

Here is the mechanic in one breath. CMS compares predicted readmissions to expected readmissions for each hospital’s patient mix. Ratio above one means more returns than the model allows. Below one earns no bonus — only relief from the stick. The stick is capped at three percent of all inpatient Medicare payments, not just the condition that tripped the alarm. A heart-failure miss can shave three percent off orthopedics revenue.

The geography is messy. New Jersey leads at sixty-five point four percent of pairs penalized. Massachusetts and Mississippi follow — dense academic markets beside rural poverty. High-resource systems do not buy immunity.

The clinical surprise is elective surgery. Hip and knee replacement posts the highest average excess ratio in the file — about one point zero zero four — nearly double the gap to the next condition when you read the fourth decimal. Joint programs are supposed to be protocol-driven. The data says otherwise.

Ownership tilts the risk. For-profit hospitals place about thirty-nine percent of pairs in the high penalty tier versus thirty-two percent for non-profits. HCA, Tenet, and peers sit in that column. The gap is real but modest — every ownership type still has a majority in the no-penalty band.

Policy fights sit outside the spreadsheet. Observation stays surged after the Affordable Care Act, shifting returns out of the inpatient column Zuckerman and colleagues documented in the New England Journal of Medicine. Mortality debates followed — Wadhera’s JAMA analysis linked the program to post-discharge death trends for heart failure. England and Canada measure readmissions without docking three percent off the entire Medicare check.

For a hospital CEO the question is not whether readmissions matter. It is whether a blunt financial penalty changes behavior faster than transitional care, pharmacy reconciliation, and home monitoring — before CMS’s TEAM bundled model makes the whole episode one invoice. This report maps where the legacy penalty still bites. The next move is operational.

SECTION THREE — OBSERVATION AND MORTALITY IN PLAIN ENGLISH.

Hospitals respond to incentives on paper. Observation stays let clinicians treat returns without inflating HRRP. That is not automatically fraud. It is metric design.

Mortality studies scared executives more than penalties. Wadhera linked HRRP rollout to heart failure mortality trends. Critics challenged causal claims. The right takeaway for a board room: track readmissions and mortality jointly. Krumholz showed decoupling at hospital level.

SECTION FOUR — INTERNATIONAL AND TEAM.

OECD countries publish readmission indicators without a three percent Medicare dock. England funds hospitals differently. Canada tracks returns provincially. U.S. executives exporting playbooks must export payment context too.

TEAM bundles hip and knee episodes for seven hundred forty-two hospitals from January first twenty twenty-six. If HRRP already hurts on joints, TEAM makes the entire episode one economic unit.

DETAILED NARRATION — PROGRAM ORIGIN.

Before HRRP, Medicare paid hospitals to fix complications of the index admission and paid again when patients returned. Jencks quantified the waste. Policymakers wanted accountability without admitting new entitlement spending for home services. HRRP was the compromise: punish excess returns, cap the punishment, expand the condition list from three to six tracks.

Each fiscal year CMS publishes supplemental files on Care Compare and in the IPPS rule. The excess readmission ratio is not intuitive to patients. It is intuitive to CFOs once they see a three percent lever on all DRGs.

DETAILED NARRATION — PEER GROUPS AND SAFETY NET.

Teaching hospitals train residents on complex cases. Safety-net hospitals admit more uninsured and Medicaid patients even when the metric is Medicare-only — because patient mix correlates across payers. CMS assigns HRRP peer groups using dual proportion to reduce apples-to-oranges comparisons. Joynt showed early penalties clustered on hospitals that could least afford consultants to game the metric.

Our chart eight name proxy is deliberately crude: government or non-profit acute hospitals with County, Public, Memorial, or Safety in the name. It is a teaching moment. If the proxy does not move the needle, your real safety-net signal is dual eligibility and post-acute access, not the sign on the building.

DETAILED NARRATION — CONDITION DEEP DIVES.

Heart failure readmissions drove policy emotion. Pneumonia and AMI were the original trio. COPD and CABG joined later. Hip and knee feel surgical, elective, controllable — yet they lead ERR in the twenty twenty-six extract. That should unsettle orthopedics strategists more than cardiology.

DETAILED NARRATION — FOR-PROFIT STRATEGY.

For-profit systems optimize throughput. Throughput without follow-up equals returns. The ownership charts do not allege inferior care. They show where CMS ratios concentrate high-tier mass. Operators can standardize discharge bundles across dozens of campuses — if leadership prioritizes it over short stay length.

DETAILED NARRATION — WHAT CHANGED IN COVID ERA DATA.

This extract includes discharges through June thirty twenty twenty-four. Pandemic-era patterns still echo in staffing and length of stay. Executives should compare their internal pre and post twenty twenty trends before blaming HRRP alone.

Zuckerman for observation. Gupta for revisits. Wadhera and Krumholz for mortality framing. Dzau and McClellan for reboot. MedPAC for payment context. OECD for international bench.
