# YOUTUBE R2 — 12 min deep dive (~1,850 words)

## Cold open

Picture two curves on one slide. The first is Alphabet's disclosed YouTube advertising revenue—eleven point two billion dollars in fiscal twenty-eighteen, thirty-six point one billion in fiscal twenty-twenty-four. The second is monthly viewership on the H3 Podcast—flat for years, tripling for nine months, then flat again at a lower plateau. This report asks what those curves have to do with each other. The honest answer: the platform can mint billions while an individual show lives or dies on casting.

## Part 1 — The platform as a filing line

Alphabet breaks out "YouTube ads" in its annual ten-K. That line is narrow. It is auction revenue on YouTube properties. It excludes subscription products that also matter strategically—YouTube Premium, YouTube Music, YouTube TV, NFL Sunday Ticket—which roll up elsewhere in Google Services.

How we know: SEC filings, not press adjectives. Fiscal twenty-twenty-two stalled at twenty-nine point two billion—about one point four percent year over year—then re-accelerated. Fiscal twenty-twenty-four's thirty-six point one billion was the fastest growth since the post-pandemic surge. What explains it? CTV watch time, brand plus direct-response demand, and Shorts inventory monetizing faster—none of which the filing itemizes per format.

Scale comparison: Netflix reported thirty-nine point zero billion in total revenue for calendar twenty-twenty-four. YouTube's thirty-six point one is ads only. Netflix is mostly membership fees. The comparison is imperfect but humbling: a creator platform's ad line rivals a global streamer's top line.

## Part 2 — The TV screen

Nielsen's Media Distributor Gauge measures U.S. television usage on television sets. Not mobile. Not desktop. In July twenty-twenty-four, Nielsen said YouTube hit ten point four percent—first time a streaming distributor led the chart. February twenty-twenty-five: eleven point six percent—YouTube's best month to date in their series. April twenty-twenty-five: twelve point four. July twenty-twenty-five: thirteen point four, with Disney at nine point four and Netflix at eight point eight.

Nielsen explicitly notes YouTube viewing in The Gauge excludes YouTube TV—the pay-TV bundle. This is the free YouTube app on Roku, Samsung, and game consoles eating scripted TV time.

April twenty-twenty-five's broader Gauge release put total streaming at forty-four point three percent of TV usage—broadcast and cable still the remainder. YouTube's rise is a share shift inside television, not proof that phones disappeared.

For long-form podcasters, CTV matters. Median H3 Podcast episodes crossed two hundred twenty minutes in twenty-twenty-four. That is a feature film, every week, on the same device where Nielsen says YouTube leads Disney.

## Part 3 — Creator payouts and splits

YouTube's Made on YouTube event in September twenty-twenty-five claimed more than one hundred billion dollars paid to creators, artists, and media companies over the prior four years. CNBC reported the figure and noted CEO Neal Mohan had cited seventy billion between twenty-twenty-one and twenty-twenty-four a year earlier—implying acceleration, not a flat payout pool.

Mechanism: the YouTube Partner Program. Google documents fifty-five percent of net revenues from ads on a creator's public watch page when the Watch Page Monetization Module is enabled. Shorts draws from subscription pools; YouTube's twenty-twenty-seven program update posts describe a forty-five percent creator share on Shorts versus fifty-five on long-form from those pools.

Goldman Sachs Research—in April twenty-twenty-three—estimated the creator economy at two hundred fifty billion dollars, projecting four hundred eighty by twenty-twenty-seven at roughly fourteen percent compound growth. They modeled YouTube Shorts ads at about seven hundred fifty million in twenty-twenty-three—two point five percent of YouTube ads—rising to about eleven point six billion in twenty-twenty-seven, twenty-six percent of YouTube ads. Goldman is not Alphabet; treat Shorts share as analyst scenario, not guidance.

Music complicates the fifty-five percent headline. Creator Music modules can shave effective share when licensed tracks clear rights.

## Part 4 — Cross-market context

TikTok: no U.S. SEC revenue line. Goldman discusses TikTok in regulatory-risk terms and sponsored-content wallets on platforms, not corporate TikTok ARPU. Compare cultures and discovery, not filings.

Twitch: Amazon bundles Twitch inside a segment that does not give us a clean annual revenue series beside YouTube ads. Live tipping and sub splits remain Twitch's story; YouTube pushes live and gifts but discloses little.

Linear TV: Nielsen shows streaming gaining share from broadcast and cable year over year. YouTube benefits as the largest single distributor on the TV glass—not as a replacement for every mobile minute.

Counterexample at platform scale: MrBeast and studio-style creators operate at per-video reach orders of magnitude above commentary podcasts. Different capital, different advertiser demand—not a moral ranking.

## Part 5 — H3 case study

Dataset: one thousand six hundred thirty-eight videos after filtering Shorts and zero-view uploads from one thousand eight hundred seventy-six pulled across four channels—h3h3productions, H3 Podcast, Hila Klein, Ethan Klein—via YouTube Data API v3, April twenty-twenty-six. Podcast durations: six hundred eighty episodes from Podchaser. Reddit: Arctic Shift pulls for r/h3h3productions and r/h3snark.

Editorial eras: Baseline twenty-seventeen through mid-twenty-twenty. Frenemies September twenty-twenty through June twenty-twenty-one—Trisha Paytas co-host. Leftovers with Hasan Piker September twenty-twenty-one through October twenty-twenty-three. Post-format phase after.

Chart one: monthly H3 Podcast views. Frenemies triples the baseline. June twenty-twenty-one breakup on camera—views back to baseline within a month. Leftovers bumps, never Frenemies.

Chart two: duration drift. Forty-two minute median in twenty-seventeen. Two hundred twenty plus in twenty-twenty-four. After twenty-twenty-two, monthly medians rarely dip below a hundred twenty minutes—the show retrained its core to expect marathons.

Chart three: Reddit indexed activity. Fan and critic subs spike on different events. April sixteen twenty-twenty-five—iDubbbz Content Cop targeting H3—r/h3snark hits three point five times its own average; fan sub barely moves. May twenty-twenty-five legal action against snark moderators: snark index collapses; fan sub continues slow decline. One community shut down, one eroding organically.

Lifetime views across channels: three point twenty-four billion in the pull—roughly zero point four per person on Earth if spread evenly, which it is not. h3h3productions median per video four point seventy-eight million versus H3 Podcast one point zero five million—the archive channel still wins per upload.

## Part 6 — Counterexamples and decision tools

Hot Ones rotates guests; the format is the IP. Frenemies was the opposite—the IP was the dyad.

H3 did not die. Post-peak millions of monthly views still flow. Correction, not extinction—in a world where YouTube pays eleven figures to creators collectively.

Executives: model co-host lifts separately from brand equity. Watch Nielsen CTV share when buying "digital video." Separate critic-community spikes from fan retention. Test whether two hundred twenty minute episodes are loyalty or friction.

## Close

YouTube's macro story in this report is verified: thirty-six billion in ads, thirteen point four percent TV-screen share in Nielsen's July twenty-twenty-five Gauge, one hundred billion plus in creator payouts per YouTube's twenty-twenty-five statement, fifty-five percent long-form watch-page share per Google help docs.

H3's micro story is also verified in our pull: a borrowed peak, a duration drift, a fan-critic split shaped by law as much as taste.

Platform growth does not hand you your co-host's audience. It only charges you rent on the audience you keep.
